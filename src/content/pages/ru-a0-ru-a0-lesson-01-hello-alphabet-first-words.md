### Hello and goodbye

| Say it | English | Polish | When |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <b>pree-VYET!</b> | Hi! | Cześć! | friends, people your age, me |
| <b>ZDRAST-vooy-tye!</b> | Hello! (polite) | Dzień dobry! | shops, offices, strangers, older people |

| <b>DO-bry VYE-cher!</b> | Good evening! | Dobry wieczór! | after about 6 p.m. |
| <b>pa-KA!</b> | Bye! | Na razie! | friends, me |

<b>Almost identical to Polish:</b> <i>DO-bry VYE-cher = dobry wieczór</i> · <i>da svee-DA-nee-ya = do widzenia</i>. <b>Watch the order:</b> Polish says <i>dzień dobry</i>, Russian says <i>DO-bry dyen'</i>.

| Say it | English | Polish |

## 6. The good news about Russian

<b>33 letters sounds like a lot. It is really 4 small groups</b> — and one of them you already know.

<b>Russian spelling is much more honest than English.</b> <i>Through, though, tough, thought</i> — English writes one thing and says another. Russian letters almost always make the same sound.

<b>You already know hundreds of Russian words</b> — from English (такси́, метро́, футбо́л, интерне́т) and from Polish (дом, вода́, ры́ба, брат). You just can't read them yet. In 20 minutes you will.

<b>Russian is an international language.</b> It is one of the six official languages of the United Nations and a common language across Central Asia, the Caucasus and the Baltic states, with large communities of speakers from Israel to Germany. One language, many countries.

<i>Today we use printed letters only. Handwritten Russian looks different — that comes later.</i>

## 7. The alphabet — 4 blocks

### Block 1 — True friends 👋

Same look, same sound. Nothing to learn.

| Letter | Sound | Example | Say it | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <b>А а</b> | a in <i>father</i> | ма́ма | MA-ma | mum |
| <b>К к</b> | k | как | kak | how |
| <b>М м</b> | m | там | tam | there |

## 8. Four reading rules

<b>1. One syllable is strong — and not like in Polish.</b> In Polish the stress is always on the second-to-last syllable. In Russian it can be anywhere: ма́ма · метро́ · до́ктор · молоко́. In learner books it is marked with ́ . Learn each word with its stress.

<b>2. Written O — but said A.</b> O sounds like <i>o</i> only when it is stressed. Everywhere else it sounds like <i>a</i>:

хорошо́ → kha-ra-SHO · молоко́ → ma-la-KO (milk) · соба́ка → sa-BA-ka (dog)

Polish never does this, so don't read Russian O the Polish way. Unstressed <b>е</b> and <b>я</b> get lighter too, close to <i>ee</i>: телефо́н → tee-lee-FON.

<b>3. Ь makes the sound before it soft</b> — like the softness in Polish ś, ć, ń: день → dyen' (compare <i>dzień</i>) · фильм → feel'm.

<b>4. At the end of a word, some letters go quiet — exactly like in Polish.</b> б → p · д → t · г → k · в → f · з → s · ж → sh

хлеб → khlyep (Polish <i>chleb</i> sounds the same way) · клуб → kloop · шокола́д → sha-ka-LAT

## 13. Careful: Polish ≠ Russian

Similar languages have traps. These five look like Polish words but mean something else.

| Russian | Say it | Means | Polish look-alike | Means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| склеп | sklyep | crypt | sklep | shop |
| запомина́ть | za-pa-mee-NAT' | to remember | zapominać | to forget |
| дива́н | dee-VAN | sofa | dywan | carpet |
| о́вощи | O-va-shshee | vegetables | owoce | fruit |
| зака́з | za-KAS | order (in a café) | zakaz | ban |

Keep the two languages apart: a separate notebook for Russian, and no switching languages in the middle of a sentence.
